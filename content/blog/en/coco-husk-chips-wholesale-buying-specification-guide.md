Coco husk chips go wrong in procurement more often than coir pith does, and not because they are harder to make. They go wrong because the words on a purchase order are usually vaguer. "Coco peat, washed, 5 kg block" is nearly unambiguous. "Coco chips, 1 x 40ft" is not a specification at all — it is a hope.

This is the buying-side companion to our guide on [what coco husk chips are and how they behave in a root zone](https://www.ppmcocopeat.com/blog/coco-husk-chips-sizes-air-porosity-crop-guide). Here we deal with the commercial half: how to specify chips so two quotes are genuinely comparable, which tests are worth paying for, how chips load into a container, and what actually sits behind a chip price.

## Why Chip Orders Go Wrong

Three structural reasons, all of them avoidable.

**Screening is a cost the supplier controls.** Producing a tight 8–16 mm grade means running material over screens, rejecting oversize and, crucially, removing fines. Every one of those steps costs money and loses sellable weight. A buyer who does not specify a grade and a maximum fines percentage has quietly told the supplier that none of it is necessary.

**Chips are slower to wash than pith.** Water has to penetrate a solid chunk rather than a dust. A factory that runs chips on the same wash cycle as pith will hand you a low EC certificate and a high EC container.

**Chips hold more calcium than pith does.** Their lignin content gives them a large cation exchange capacity, so an unbuffered chip strips calcium and magnesium out of your feed for weeks. The symptom appears in the crop, not in the invoice.

## The Six Axes of a Chip Specification

Write all six into the purchase order. Anything you leave out is a decision you have handed to someone whose interests are not yours.

| Axis | What to state | What happens if you omit it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Material and blend | Pure chips, or pith:chip ratio by volume | You receive whatever ratio is convenient |
| Size grade | 8–16 mm or 15–25 mm, with tolerance and max fines % | You receive ungraded material with fines |
| Treatment grade | Unwashed / washed low EC / fully buffered, with EC ceiling **and** extraction method | You receive an EC number you cannot interpret |
| pH band | Typically 5.5 – 6.8 | Out-of-band pH arrives and is blamed on your feed |
| Moisture and impurities | Moisture 10–15% max, sand and impurities under 3% | You pay ocean freight on water and sand |
| Format and packaging | Block or bale size, compressed or loose, palletised or floor-loaded, marks | Handling surprises on arrival |

Two of these deserve a closer look.

### Size grade is not just a number

A grade is a number _plus a tolerance plus a fines cap_. "8–16 mm" on its own permits a batch that is 30% dust by weight, because the dust is technically under 16 mm. State it properly: 8–16 mm, minimum 90% within grade, maximum 5% passing a 5 mm screen. Now the sentence means something and a lab can check it.

### EC without a method is not a number

An EC figure depends entirely on how the sample was extracted. A 1:5 dilution, a 1:1.5 dilution and a pour-through all produce different values from the same material, and the differences are large enough to turn a failing batch into a passing one. Specify the method alongside the ceiling, and require that the batch report uses the same method. The full logic is in the [low EC vs. high EC procurement guide](https://www.ppmcocopeat.com/blog/low-ec-vs-high-ec-coco-peat-procurement-guide).

## Washed Is Not Buffered

This single confusion probably costs growers more money than any other in coir procurement.

- **Washing** flushes soluble salts out with fresh water. It lowers EC. It does nothing about the exchange sites.
- **Buffering** soaks the material in a calcium solution — normally calcium nitrate — so the exchange sites are saturated with calcium before the chips ever meet your feed. It is what stops the substrate from stealing calcium and magnesium from your nutrient solution and releasing sodium in return.

Chips can be thoroughly washed and still be completely unbuffered. For hydroponics, and for any pure-chip medium where there is no pith to dilute the effect, "washed low EC" is not sufficient. Specify **fully calcium-buffered** and ask what concentration and contact time were used.

| Grade | Typical EC expectation | Suitable for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Unwashed / high EC | High, variable | Mushroom substrate, animal bedding, erosion control |
| Washed low EC | Low, to a stated ceiling | Ornamentals, nursery stock, tolerant field crops |
| Fully buffered | Low and stable under feed | Hydroponics, high-value greenhouse crops, pure-chip media |

## Tests Worth Paying For

Ask for a **batch-specific** report, not a factory certificate with last year's date on it. A useful report covers:

1. **EC**, with the extraction method stated.
2. **pH**, same sample.
3. **Sodium and chloride** individually — these are what actually damage a crop, and a low total EC can still hide a high sodium fraction.
4. **Moisture content** — you are paying freight on this.
5. **Particle size distribution**, including the percentage passing a 5 mm screen.
6. **Sand and impurity content**.

For a buffered product, also ask for the calcium and magnesium figures. If a supplier cannot produce a batch report, that is information too.

## Sampling Before You Commit a Season

The cheapest insurance in this trade is a paid sample from the same production run you intend to buy, tested by you or by an independent lab, before the container is booked. Hydrate it, measure EC yourself, screen a kilogram to check the grade, and grow a short trial if the crop is high value.

A sample that arrives from a different batch than the one you will receive tells you nothing. Put the batch reference in writing.

## How Chips Load Into a Container

Chips compress, but not like pith. Fine pith compacts to roughly a fifteenth of its loose volume, which is why a 40ft HC of 5 kg pith blocks carries such an enormous number of rehydrated litres. A chip is already a low-density rigid chunk and resists compaction, so the same container of chips delivers materially fewer usable litres.

The practical consequences:

- **Do not apply pith rules of thumb to chip volumes.** Ask the supplier for stated expanded litres per block or bale for that specific product, and build your landed cost from that number.
- **Blended blocks behave in between.** A 70:30 pith:chip block compresses better than pure chips and worse than pure pith.
- **Weight or volume will cap you, depending on the product.** Compressed chip blocks usually hit the container's volume limit before its weight limit. Confirm which, because it changes how you negotiate.

The loading and weight arithmetic for a full container, along with the difference between volume-out and weight-out, is worked through in the [40ft HC container loading guide](https://www.ppmcocopeat.com/blog/5kg-coco-peat-blocks-40ft-container-loading-guide).

## What Actually Drives a Chip Price

A chip quote is four prices stacked on top of each other:

1. **Raw material** — husk availability and the season.
2. **Processing** — cutting, screening to grade, and rejecting out-of-grade material.
3. **Treatment** — washing (water, time, tank capacity) and buffering (calcium nitrate, contact time).
4. **Freight** — driven by compressed volume, which for chips is less favourable than for pith.

This is why a quote that is far below the others is usually not a better deal but a different product: unwashed instead of washed, ungraded instead of screened, or wetter than the moisture cap you specified.

Compare on **cost per usable litre delivered into the growing position** — the landed cost of the material plus the labour, water and chemicals you spend at your end — rather than on price per kilogram. The FOB-versus-CIF mechanics are set out in the [coco peat price guide](https://www.ppmcocopeat.com/blog/coco-peat-price-fob-cif-landed-cost-guide), and they apply to chips unchanged.

## Documents and Compliance

Chips carry the same documentation set as any other coir substrate: commercial invoice and packing list, bill of lading, certificate of origin, a phytosanitary certificate, and a fumigation certificate where the destination requires one. Coir products normally sit under HS heading 5305; confirm the exact sub-heading and any import permit with your own broker before the container is booked, because getting it wrong is a demurrage problem, not a paperwork problem. The detail is in the [India import compliance guide](https://www.ppmcocopeat.com/blog/import-coco-coir-from-india-phytosanitary-compliance-guide).

## Supplier Red Flags

- A quote that does not restate your size grade back to you.
- An EC figure with no extraction method.
- "Washed and buffered" used as a single phrase, with no buffering detail.
- A factory certificate offered in place of a batch report.
- A sample that cannot be tied to a production batch.
- Unwillingness to state expanded litres per block.
- A price well below the market with no explanation of what was left out.

None of these is proof of bad faith. Every one of them is a question you should ask before the container is booked rather than after it arrives.

## A First-Order Protocol

1. Write the six-axis specification into the purchase order.
2. Request a paid sample with a batch reference.
3. Test EC, pH, sodium and grade yourself, or through an independent lab.
4. Agree the batch report format and the EC extraction method in writing.
5. Order a trial container before committing a season's volume.
6. Re-test on arrival and compare against the batch report.

Follow that once and you have a supplier relationship you can repeat. Skip it and you are buying a new product every time.
